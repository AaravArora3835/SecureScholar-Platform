Lesson 1 — What is Phishing?

Imagine getting an email that says your Netflix account will be deleted unless you "verify your payment information" in the next 24 hours. You click the link, enter your credit card details, and… congratulations, you just got phished.

Phishing is when someone pretends to be a trusted company, friend, or authority figure to trick you into giving them sensitive information—like passwords, credit card numbers, or Social Security numbers. It's called "phishing" because hackers cast a wide net hoping someone will bite the bait.

And it works. According to the Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report, 36% of all data breaches involve phishing. That's more than one in three cyberattacks. Why? Because phishing targets the weakest link in any security system: humans.

Here's a real example: You get a text saying "Your package can't be delivered. Click here to reschedule." It looks legitimate—there's even a tracking number. But the link takes you to a fake website designed to steal your information. Within minutes, hackers have access to your accounts.

So why do smart people fall for this? Psychology. Phishing emails use urgency ("Act now or lose access!"), authority ("This is your IT department"), and fear ("Suspicious activity detected"). When you're stressed or distracted, your brain takes shortcuts—and that's exactly when phishing works.

The good news? Once you know what to look for, phishing becomes much easier to spot. And that's what this module is all about: giving you the tools to protect yourself, your family, and your digital identity.

Aarav's Note:

I almost fell for a phishing email last year. It looked exactly like a message from my school's IT department, asking me to "verify my student portal login." The email address was perfect, the logo looked right, and I was stressed about an assignment deadline. I hovered over the link at the last second and noticed the URL was totally different from our school's actual website—it was some random .com domain instead of our .edu address.

That one moment of checking saved me from giving hackers my school login, which would've exposed my grades, personal info, and possibly my family's data if I reused that password anywhere else. Now I always hover before I click. It takes two extra seconds, but it's the easiest way to catch fake emails.
